The county elections dispatch, week of the canvass

Elections officials in Warren County finished their canvass on Wednesday, closing out a
count that had been held open by a larger-than-usual pile of provisional ballots. Staff
flagged 185 provisional ballots for identification review, most of them cast by voters
who had moved within the county since the last election.

Provisional voting exists because the rolls are never quite current. A voter who has
moved, married, or been struck from a list in error can still cast a ballot, and the
clerk decides afterwards whether it counts. Most counties in the state resolve a handful
of these each cycle. A few resolve many more, and the reasons are usually mundane: a
university term that begins a week before the registration deadline, a large employer
that relocated a shift, a stretch of new housing that has not yet been assigned to a
precinct on anybody's map.

The mechanics are unglamorous. A clerk pulls the envelope, matches the signature against
whatever the county holds on file, and either accepts the ballot or writes down a reason
for not doing so. Where the signature is the only question, the voter is written to and
given a window to answer. Where the address is the question, the answer usually lives in
another office's records, and the wait is for a person rather than for a form.

None of this is fast, and none of it is meant to be. The statutory deadline sets the
outer bound and the clerk's office works backwards from it, which is why a canvass that
looks stalled from outside is often exactly on schedule from inside. Observers from both
parties sit in on the review, and the log of every decision is public once the count is
certified.

What is new this cycle is the volume of address questions rather than signature ones.
The clerk attributes that to a redistricting map that moved several blocks between
precincts without moving anybody's house, which produced a run of ballots cast in good
faith at the wrong table. Those are the easiest to resolve and the most tedious to count,
because each one has to be matched against the old boundary as well as the new one.

A second question the office fielded all week was why the count could not simply be
published as it stood and amended later. The answer is that a certified total is a legal
object rather than a running tally: once it is signed it starts the clock on every
challenge, recount and audit that follows, and a total amended afterwards restarts none
of them. So the office publishes an unofficial tally, says plainly that it is unofficial,
and waits.

The audit that follows certification is separate again, and it is the part most people
never see. A random sample of precincts is counted by hand against the machine totals,
and a discrepancy above a published threshold widens the sample rather than triggering a
recount outright. In a county this size the sample is a few thousand ballots and the work
takes an afternoon. It has not widened here in six years, which the clerk offers less as
a boast than as an explanation of why nobody outside the building has heard of it.

Reviewers cleared the backlog over four days. The clerk's office said the pace was steady
and that no ballot was set aside for want of time.

The board voted to certify the count on Thursday evening, with all five members present.
The certified totals moved no race by more than a dozen votes.
