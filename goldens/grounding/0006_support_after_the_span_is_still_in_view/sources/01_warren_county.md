Warren County opens its provisional-ballot review

The board voted to certify the count on Thursday evening, with all five members present.
The certified totals moved no race by more than a dozen votes, and the clerk said the
canvass had run to schedule from the first day to the last.

Certification is a legal object rather than a running tally. Once it is signed it starts
the clock on every challenge, recount and audit that follows, and a total amended
afterwards restarts none of them. So the office publishes an unofficial tally, says
plainly that it is unofficial, and waits for the review to finish before signing anything.

Provisional voting exists because the rolls are never quite current. A voter who has
moved, married, or been struck from a list in error can still cast a ballot, and the clerk
decides afterwards whether it counts. Most counties in the state resolve a handful of
these each cycle; a few resolve many more, and the reasons are usually mundane.

Staff flagged 185 provisional ballots for identification review this cycle, most of them
cast by voters who had moved within the county since the last election.

The mechanics are unglamorous. A clerk pulls the envelope, matches the signature against
whatever the county holds on file, and either accepts the ballot or writes down a reason
for not doing so. Where the signature is the only question, the voter is written to and
given a window to answer. Where the address is the question, the answer usually lives in
another office's records, and the wait is for a person rather than for a form.

None of this is fast, and none of it is meant to be. The statutory deadline sets the outer
bound and the clerk's office works backwards from it, which is why a canvass that looks
stalled from outside is often exactly on schedule from inside. Observers from both parties
sit in on the review, and the log of every decision is public once the count is certified.

What is new this cycle is the volume of address questions rather than signature ones. The
clerk attributes that to a redistricting map that moved several blocks between precincts
without moving anybody's house, which produced a run of ballots cast in good faith at the
wrong table. Those are the easiest to resolve and the most tedious to count, because each
one has to be matched against the old boundary as well as the new one.

The audit that follows certification is separate again, and it is the part most people
never see. A random sample of precincts is counted by hand against the machine totals, and
a discrepancy above a published threshold widens the sample rather than triggering a
recount outright. In a county this size the sample is a few thousand ballots and the work
takes an afternoon. It has not widened here in six years, which the clerk offers less as a
boast than as an explanation of why nobody outside the building has heard of it.
